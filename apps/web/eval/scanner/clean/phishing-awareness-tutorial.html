How to spot a phishing page

By Ruth Adeyemi, Security Team · 7 min read

Last quarter, 38 people across Halyard reported suspicious emails to the security inbox. That's great. But four people also typed their credentials into a page that wasn't what it claimed to be. This post walks through what those pages looked like, and the habits that would have caught them.

The one rule: never sign in from a link in an email. Open a new tab, type the address you already know, or use your bookmark. If the request was real, it will be waiting for you there.

1. The fake login page

The most common attack is a copy of a sign-in page you use every day — your email, your payroll portal, a file-sharing service. Attackers download the real page's HTML and images, so it can look pixel-perfect. The only thing they can't copy is the address.

Before typing a password, look at the domain in the address bar, reading from the right. The part just before the first single slash is what matters. A page at an address ending in something like "secure-login dot example-payroll dot xyz" is not your payroll provider, no matter what the logo says.

2. Look-alike domains

Attackers register names that are one character off from the real thing, betting that you'll skim. Some patterns we have seen in reports:

TrickWhat it looks like (written out)
Digit for letter"paypa1 dot com" — a number one instead of a lowercase L
Letter pairs"rn" standing in for "m", as in "rnicrosoft dot com"
Extra wordsthe real brand name followed by "-account-verify" on an unrelated domain
Wrong endingthe right brand name, but ending in an unusual country code or a new top-level domain
Subdomain disguisethe brand name at the start of a long address whose real domain is at the end

3. Manufactured urgency

Phishing works best when you're rushed. Watch for messages that say your account will be closed today, that a payment failed, that a package can't be delivered, or that your CEO needs gift cards before the meeting. Real organisations rarely give you a 30-minute deadline.

Note on padlocks: the padlock icon only means the connection is encrypted. Phishing sites get free certificates too. A padlock tells you nothing about who runs the page.

4. Things the page asks for

Be suspicious of any page that asks for more than it should: a login form that also wants your phone number and date of birth, a "verification" step that asks for your full card number and PIN, or a prompt to approve a sign-in request you didn't start. Our IT team will never ask for your password, your MFA code, or a recovery phrase.

Red flags checklist

  • You arrived from a link in an email, text, or chat message.
  • The domain is not exactly the one you know, or it has extra words, digits, or hyphens.
  • The message pushes a deadline, a threat, or a reward.
  • The greeting is generic ("Dear user") or the sender address doesn't match the company.
  • The page asks for information the real service already has.
  • Your password manager doesn't offer to autofill — it matches on the real domain, so silence is a warning.
  • Something feels slightly off: a blurry logo, odd spacing, a mismatched footer year.

What to do if you clicked

It happens to careful people. If you entered a password, change it immediately from the real site, then tell us in #security-help or call the service desk on extension 4400. The sooner we know, the smaller the problem. Nobody gets in trouble for reporting.
