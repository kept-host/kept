How Valmere learned to ride: a decade of cycling growth

By Corinne Adeyemi · Data from the Valmere municipal bike counters, 2015–2025

Ten years ago, fewer than one in twenty trips in Valmere was made by bike. The city was built around a ring road and a handful of busy arterials, and cycling was something you did on weekends along the river — if at all.

Today, bikes carry more commuters across the Old Bridge than cars do at rush hour. This is how that happened, in four charts.

The growth wasn't steady. It came in jumps — and each jump lines up with a stretch of new protected lanes opening. Painted lanes did little; physically separated ones changed behaviour within months.

"We stopped asking whether people wanted to cycle and started asking whether they felt safe doing it. The answer to the second question explained everything." — Tomasz Ilić, Valmere transport planner

Who is riding has changed too. In 2015, the typical cyclist was a man in his thirties. Now the split is close to even, and the fastest-growing group is riders over 55.

Finally, the seasons. Winter used to empty the lanes. Since the city began clearing protected lanes before roads after snowfall, winter ridership has held at roughly 60% of summer levels.

None of this required a technological breakthrough. It took paint, then concrete, then patience — and a city willing to measure what it was doing.
